Alessandro Fassò, Jacopo Rodeschini, Alessandro Fusta Moro, Qendrim Shaboviq, Paolo Maranzano, Michela Cameletti, Francesco Finazzi, Natalia Golini, Rosaria Ignaccolo, Philipp Otto (2023). Agrimonia: a dataset on livestock, meteorology and air quality in the Lombardy region, Italy. Scientific Data.

Summary

Agricultural ammonia can contribute to secondary particulate matter, but the underlying air-quality observations, emissions estimates, weather and agricultural records are collected at different scales. Fassò and co-authors (2023) bring these sources together in the Agrimonia dataset. The harmonised panel contains daily records for 2016–2021 at 141 ground-level monitoring stations in Lombardy and a 0.3° buffer around the region.

The publication is a data descriptor. It documents the sources, the transformations needed to obtain a station-day panel, and checks on the resulting variables. The linked data support pollution mapping, prediction at unobserved locations and studies of associations between farming activity, weather and air quality. The emissions and livestock records retain the interpretation imposed by their original geographical and temporal resolution.

From source data to station-day records

The air-quality measurements come from regional and European monitoring networks, while meteorological fields and emissions inventories come from Copernicus. Livestock counts are drawn from the Italian zootechnical registry, and land-cover information includes CORINE. The workflow aggregates hourly values and aligns gridded or area-level information with station locations. Some slower-moving series, including livestock counts, are interpolated in time, so their daily resolution reflects the harmonisation process.

Pollutant coverage is uneven because stations observe different sets of pollutants. The descriptor explains how problematic measurements and missing values were handled and provides metadata on imputation uncertainty. These details matter if the analysis relies on extremes, comparisons among pollutants or station-based validation. The Zenodo record provides the data and metadata, and the project repository provides the processing code.

Predicting PM₂.₅ at held-out stations

A separate study by Otto and co-authors (2024) compares a hidden dynamic geostatistical model, a generalised additive mixed model and random forest spatiotemporal kriging. Using PM₂.₅ observations at 49 stations from 2016 to 2020, the authors evaluated predictions by leaving out one station at a time. The hidden dynamic geostatistical model had the lowest average RMSE in that comparison (6.31 µg/m³).

The more flexible models were useful for exploring nonlinear relationships with the covariates. Together the analyses illustrate two uses of the same harmonised panel: assessing accuracy at unobserved stations and identifying possible improvements to the model for large-scale variation. An analysis of what would happen under an emissions intervention would require additional identification assumptions.

Research interests

I am interested in statistical data science and statistical methodology for data in multidimensional spaces, e.g., geo-referenced data. Most of my papers have one theory in common - Tobler's first law of geography: "everything is related to everything else, but near things are more related than distant things."
