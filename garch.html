Philipp Otto, Wolfgang Schmid (2023). A general framework for spatial GARCH models. Statistical Papers.

What problem does this model address?

Spatial GARCH allows the local volatility scale to depend on scales at connected locations as well as on observed shocks. The spatial weights specify which connections enter the model. This lets researchers study how volatility can propagate across a network within a single observation period.

When should this model be used?

Use this framework when local shocks and volatility at connected sites may both contribute to the scale of a response. Specify which neighbours can interact and check the existence conditions for the chosen model and weights.

Summary

Otto and Schmid (2023) develop a framework for spatial and spatiotemporal generalised autoregressive conditional heteroscedasticity (GARCH). Its spatial GARCH specification links local scales to neighbouring squared shocks and to neighbouring scales. The framework includes spatial ARCH, symmetric spatial log-GARCH and, with temporal weights, standard time-series GARCH.

Main contributions

  • Separate weight matrices describe interactions through observed shock magnitudes and through the scale process.
  • A fixed-point formulation gives conditions for existence and uniqueness.
  • The paper derives a nonlinear least-squares estimator and illustrates it in simulations and an application to Berlin property prices.

When do neighbouring scales matter?

A spatial ARCH term can describe clustering associated with shocks at neighbouring locations. The additional GARCH term allows scale changes to propagate through the weights even where a neighbouring realised shock is small.

Contemporaneous spatial spillovers

Unlike lagged time-series GARCH, spatial GARCH can link sites observed in the same period. Reciprocal links imply a simultaneous system. Interpret its spillovers as modelled dependence given the chosen weights.

Spatial GARCH and log-GARCH equations

Let \(Y^{(2)}=Y\odot Y\) denote squared residuals, \(h\) the vector of squared local scales and \(E=\operatorname{diag}(\varepsilon_i^2)\). The spatial GARCH model writes

\[Y=\operatorname{diag}(h)^{1/2}\varepsilon,\qquad h=\alpha+W_1Y^{(2)}+W_2h.\]

Substituting \(Y^{(2)}=Eh\) gives \(h=(I-W_1 E-W_2)^{-1}\alpha\) under the relevant existence conditions. With nonnegative weights and positive baseline scales, \(\lVert W_1E+W_2\rVert<1\) in a submultiplicative matrix norm is sufficient. Setting \(W_2=0\) gives spatial ARCH.

In the symmetric spatial log-GARCH example, \(\log h=\alpha+W_1\log Y^{(2)}+W_2\log h\), with elementwise logarithms and nonzero innovations. A sufficient condition for a unique solution is \(\lVert W_1+W_2\rVert<1\).

Estimation by nonlinear least squares

The paper develops nonlinear least squares based on the log-squared transformation. A common specification is \(W_1=\rho W_1^*\) and \(W_2=\lambda W_2^*\), with known weight structures and estimated coefficients. The consistency result uses identification and regularity conditions.

Diagnostics and interpretation

  • Inspect squared residuals after fitting a plausible mean model. Spatial clustering in their magnitudes motivates comparing alternative mean and scale structures.
  • Compare weights based on adjacency, distance or an application-specific network. Check existence conditions and inspect standardised residuals after fitting.
  • In simultaneous settings, interpret \(h_i\) as a squared local scale. A directional model with an appropriate ordering permits a conditional-variance interpretation.

Application to Berlin property prices

The paper studies long-run property price changes across Berlin postcode areas from 1995 to 2014. After fitting a spatial autoregressive mean with regressors and spatial GARCH errors, the squared standardised residuals no longer showed significant spatial autocorrelation in the reported diagnostic. The fitted scale highlighted areas with larger local uncertainty.
