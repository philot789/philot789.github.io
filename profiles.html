Philipp Otto, Andreas Piter, Rik Gijsman (2021). Statistical analysis of beach profiles – A spatiotemporal functional approach. Coastal Engineering.

Summary

Coastal monitoring programmes record beach elevation along transects, often at uneven intervals in space and time. Some surveys cover only the dry beach or only the submerged part. Otto, Piter and Gijsman (2021) apply a functional spatiotemporal model in which each measurement contributes at its recorded cross-shore position. A partly surveyed transect can still inform estimation and help predict the missing portion of a profile.

The paper uses two case studies from Sylt, Germany. One examines how dependence changes from nearshore to offshore in submerged profiles. The other estimates the shape of the beach following storms and nourishment in the emerged zone. The cases illustrate how the same statistical framework can support different questions about monitoring and beach morphology.

Modelling incomplete transects

The response is an elevation curve indexed by distance from the shoreline. B-splines express the average shape and how covariate effects vary along the profile. A hidden dynamic geostatistical component allows nearby transects to share information and represents persistence across survey times. The model separates this dependence from measurement and modelling error. Evaluating the basis at the actual survey points avoids requiring an identical grid for every transect.

The fitted model can interpolate a profile at an unsurveyed alongshore location and predict a future profile under specified conditions. The authors use subsampling to make fitting feasible and hold out groups of transects to assess interpolation. Errors can then be examined as a function of cross-shore distance, which is more informative for survey planning than one average error for the whole curve.

What the Sylt surveys show

For the submerged profiles at Puan Klent, estimated temporal dependence weakened offshore. The authors connect that pattern to survey design and discuss more frequent measurements nearshore. The finding is specific to the temporal dependence in this dataset. Choosing survey intervals also requires considering the precision needed for the particular part of the profile.

For the emerged profiles at List, the selected model used indicators for major wave conditions and nourishment. Following events with wave heights above 5 m, the fitted storm effect amounted to about 0.2–0.7 m of elevation loss across parts of the profile. The estimated changes describe a difference in profile shape, so the magnitude is tied to cross-shore position and should be read together with the uncertainty in the fitted curves.
