Philipp Otto, Osman Doğan, Süleyman Taşpınar, Wolfgang Schmid, Anil K. Bera (2025). Spatial and spatiotemporal volatility models: A review. Journal of Economic Surveys.

Summary

Otto, Doğan, Taşpınar, Schmid and Bera (2025) review models for dependence in volatility across locations and over time. The starting point is a familiar feature of financial time series: large shocks tend to occur in clusters. If risk also varies across connected sites, dependence can appear in squared or absolute deviations even when the signed deviations show little correlation. The review works from time-series and multivariate foundations towards models that encode geographical or network structure explicitly.

The survey distinguishes two broad families by how their volatility is generated. ARCH and GARCH specifications build the scale from observed shocks and, in GARCH, other scales. Stochastic volatility models give the scale its own latent process. This choice determines the interpretation of a volatility cluster, the role of observed past data and the estimation methods available.

Comparing models for spatial volatility

Spatial weights encode which locations can interact. Contemporaneous links can create a simultaneous system, so well-defined solutions depend on the weights and transformation. In spatiotemporal data, further terms can represent persistence at a site or transmission from other sites with a lag. The review covers level-scale and logarithmic specifications, including models that use log-squared responses to give the transformed system a spatial autoregressive form.

In a stochastic volatility model, innovations to an unobserved log-scale process can generate a cluster of high variability. Inference must then integrate over or estimate the latent states. The review discusses likelihood and moment-based approaches for particular ARCH/GARCH specifications and Bayesian computation for several stochastic volatility models. The appendices give technical details for selected estimators and samplers.

Choosing a specification

A useful first diagnostic is whether absolute or squared residuals still cluster after fitting the mean. If the question concerns how observed shocks relate to neighbouring scales, a spatial ARCH or GARCH model makes that link explicit. If the scientific question calls for a separate evolving risk process, stochastic volatility provides a different interpretation. The sampling design also matters. Repeated observations can distinguish persistence from same-period spatial association when the model and data support both.

The review identifies open problems, including asymmetric responses to shocks, direction-dependent spatial transmission and more flexible structures for multivariate volatility. For a worked example of same-period scale feedback and its diagnostics, see the spatial GARCH application to Berlin prices.
