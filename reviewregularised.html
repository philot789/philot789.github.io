What problem does this review address?

Choose the validation split to match the prediction task. Predicting the future at observed sites calls for a temporal split. Predicting in an unobserved region calls for holding out locations together. The same distinction matters when tuning a regularisation penalty.

When should this review be used?

Use this review when selecting covariates, estimating a large dependence structure or tuning a penalty for georeferenced observations. Define whether predictions will concern new sites, future periods or both before choosing validation folds.

Summary

Otto, Fassò and Maranzano (2024) review regularised estimation for geostatistical and spatial econometric models. The survey covers variable selection, regularisation of covariance and precision matrices, and estimation of spatial interactions. It connects penalty selection with validation designs tailored to prediction at new places, future times, or both.

Main contributions

The general estimation problem combines a loss and a complexity penalty, \[\widehat\theta=\arg\min_\theta\{L(\theta;X,y)+P(\theta,\theta_0,\lambda)\}.\] The parameter \(\lambda\) controls the strength of the penalty towards a reference \(\theta_0\), often zero for sparsity.

  • For fixed effects, LASSO and related penalties can select predictors or groups of terms.
  • For geostatistical dependence, covariance tapering and sparse precision estimates constrain different parts of the model. Low-rank and Vecchia approximations address computation.
  • For spatial autoregression, penalisation can select among candidate weights or help estimate a structured weights matrix.

Why this approach is needed

Big geospatial datasets require models that are interpretable and computationally feasible. Regularisation provides both: it controls complexity, selects relevant structure, and enables scalable inference in mixed-effects geostatistics and spatial autoregression. The survey unifies these ideas and connects them to CV procedures tailored for dependence.

Why regularise a spatiotemporal model?

Regularisation trades bias against sampling variability and can make estimation feasible with many predictors or dependence parameters. The preferred degree of regularisation can differ across prediction tasks, so tune it using a split that reflects how the model will be deployed.

Regularisation highlights

  • Fixed-effects selection: high-dimensional regressors via (adaptive) LASSO; extensions to functional predictors with penalised splines.
  • Covariance/precision structure: tapering (distance-based zeros), sparse precision via graphical LASSO, and low-rank Cholesky/Vecchia for large N.
  • Spatial weights: boosting or LASSO to choose among candidate \(W_k\); constrained procedures to estimate \(W\) (identifiability via structure or sparsity).

Cross-validation for new sites and future periods

  • Forecasting at observed sites. Hold out a later period and fit using only observations available at the forecast origin. Rolling-origin evaluation can cover several origins and horizons.
  • Predicting at unobserved sites. Hold out complete locations or spatial blocks. Add a buffer when the intended predictions are distant from the training locations.
  • Forecasting at unobserved sites. Hold out both the target sites and the target period so that the split matches the information available in deployment.

Random folds can produce optimistic estimates when nearby or consecutive observations make the task easier than intended. Blocking and buffering can reduce dependence across folds. They do not guarantee independence.

Tuning penalties and measuring performance

Fit preprocessing steps and select the penalty within the training data for each split. When estimating the performance of a tuned procedure, use an outer holdout or nested resampling that mirrors the intended prediction task.

Applications in spatiotemporal statistics

Environmental monitoring (geostatistical interpolation and forecasting), regional economics (panel SAR/CAR with variable selection), health and epidemiology (areal models with sparse precision), transportation and networks (directional weights), and remote sensing (functional predictors and basis selection).
