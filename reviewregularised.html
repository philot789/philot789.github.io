Philipp Otto, Alessandro Fassò, Paolo Maranzano (2024). A review of regularised estimation methods and cross-validation in spatiotemporal statistics. Statistics Surveys.

Summary

Regularisation helps when a spatiotemporal model has many potential predictors or a dependence structure with too many parameters to estimate precisely. Otto, Fassò and Maranzano (2024) review how penalties and related constraints are used in geostatistics and spatial econometrics. They distinguish regularising the mean, the covariance or precision matrix, and the spatial weights matrix. These operations target different scientific questions even when they use similar optimisation tools.

The paper also reviews how to choose tuning parameters and evaluate predictions when observations are dependent. A validation split defines the prediction task: interpolation near known observations, transfer to unobserved sites or regions, forecasting beyond the observed period, or prediction across both space and time.

What the penalty changes

Penalised estimation combines a measure of fit with a penalty on model complexity. Ridge penalties shrink coefficients, LASSO penalties can set coefficients to zero, and group penalties can select an entire block together. For example, a spatiotemporal regression can select a covariate together with its associated basis coefficients or lag terms if those parameters form a scientifically meaningful group. The penalty introduces bias in exchange for potentially lower estimation variability.

Covariance tapering induces sparsity by attenuating or removing covariance at larger distances, which can reduce computational cost. Penalising a precision matrix instead concerns conditional associations, while penalising a SAR weights matrix selects directed terms in an autoregressive equation. A zero therefore carries a different interpretation in each model. Methods for those matrices also have to respect the relevant conditions, such as positive definiteness or invertibility.

Validation for space and time

If the task is to interpolate at an unobserved monitoring station, holding out all observations from that station makes the test resemble the intended use. Spatial blocks or buffers can better represent transfer across a larger gap. If the task is forecasting, forward validation uses only observations available at each forecast origin. To assess a model intended for both new places and future periods, the holdout must reflect both restrictions.

Random folds can be informative for some interpolation tasks where nearby data are part of the information actually available at prediction time. Those same folds can give optimistic estimates for transfer or forecasting. The choice of block size, gap and forecast horizon should reflect the dependence and the deployment setting. When estimating the error of a tuning procedure, repeat the tuning inside each training split so that test outcomes stay outside model selection.
