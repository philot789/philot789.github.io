Philipp Otto, Wolfgang Schmid, Robert Garthoff (2018). Generalised spatial and spatiotemporal autoregressive conditional heteroscedasticity. Spatial Statistics.

Summary

A map of residuals can look unstructured even when nearby locations tend to have unusually large residuals at the same time. Spatial autoregressive conditional heteroscedasticity (spARCH) describes this clustering in the magnitude of deviations. Otto, Schmid and Garthoff (2018) extend the ARCH idea from time series to spatial and spatiotemporal data, where the squared deviations that enter a site's scale equation can be contemporaneous.

The paper gives conditions for the process to exist, derives likelihood-based estimation and combines a spatial autoregressive mean with spARCH errors. Simulations examine the behaviour of the estimators. In an application to lung and bronchus cancer mortality across US counties, the model accounts for spatial dependence in squared residuals that persisted after the authors fitted a spatial mean model.

How spatial ARCH works

Write the residuals as \(\xi=\operatorname{diag}(h)^{1/2}\varepsilon\), where \(\varepsilon\) is a vector of innovations and \(h\) contains the squared local scales. The basic scale equation is

\[h=\alpha+W(\xi\odot\xi),\] where \(\alpha\) represents the baseline, \(\odot\) denotes elementwise multiplication and \(W\) specifies which locations contribute to each site's scale. Squaring gives large positive and negative residuals the same role. The mean equation can use a different spatial weights matrix from the scale equation.

In a time series, earlier shocks are known when the next scale is computed. With reciprocal spatial links, residuals and scales instead form a simultaneous system. The paper treats the existence of a nonnegative solution explicitly and distinguishes general spatial weights from oriented, triangular weights. In a simultaneous model, interpret \(h_i\) as a local squared scale. Its interpretation as a conditional variance depends on the conditioning set and the ordering.

Evidence from US county mortality

The paper analyses average lung and bronchus cancer mortality for 2008–2012 across 3,108 US counties. It compares ordinary regression, a spatial autoregressive (SAR) mean model and a SAR model with spARCH errors. In the SAR fit, Moran's \(I\) was close to zero for signed residuals, while the squared residuals remained positively spatially autocorrelated (\(I=0.321\), Table 2). With the spARCH error model, Moran's \(I\) for the squared standardised residuals was about 0.007.

This comparison shows why a diagnostic on signed residuals alone can miss spatial heterogeneity relevant to uncertainty. The application models departures from county-level expected mortality under the selected covariates and weights. The estimated scale describes geographical variation in the size of those departures.
