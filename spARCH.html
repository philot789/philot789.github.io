Philipp Otto, Wolfgang Schmid, Robert Garthoff (2018). Generalised spatial and spatiotemporal autoregressive conditional heteroscedasticity. Spatial Statistics.

What problem does this model address?

Spatial ARCH links the scale of a response at one location to squared responses at connected locations. Large deviations of either sign can therefore occur near one another even when a map of the mean shows little spatial structure.

When should this model be used?

Use spatial ARCH when a mean model leaves clusters of unusually large or small residual magnitudes and a weights matrix can represent plausible links between locations. A map of squared residuals can help identify this pattern.

Summary

Spatial autoregressive conditional heteroscedasticity (spARCH) models dependence in the magnitude of shocks across locations. Otto, Schmid and Garthoff (2018) define a spatial and spatiotemporal ARCH process, develop likelihood-based estimation and combine it with a spatial autoregression for the mean. Their application examines lung-cancer mortality across 3,108 US counties.

Main contributions

  • The spatial weights matrix specifies which squared shocks enter the local scale equation.
  • The likelihood accounts for simultaneous interactions between locations.
  • A spatial autoregressive mean can be combined with spARCH errors to model separate patterns in the level and scale of the response.

Why model spatial dependence in volatility?

A mean model describes where the expected response is high or low. Residuals can still show geographical clustering in magnitude after the mean has been modelled. An ARCH error process describes that second pattern.

From temporal ARCH to spatial ARCH

Temporal ARCH uses earlier squared shocks. Spatial ARCH uses squared shocks at linked sites, which can be observed at the same time. A large positive or negative deviation enters the scale equation through its square.

Spatial ARCH model

For a response vector \(Y\), mean model \(m(X,\theta)\), and innovation vector \(\varepsilon\), write

\[Y=m(X,\theta)+\xi,\qquad \xi=\operatorname{diag}(h)^{1/2}\varepsilon,\qquad h=\alpha+W(\xi\odot\xi).\]

Here \(h\) gives the squared local scales, \(\odot\) denotes elementwise multiplication, \(\alpha\) gives baseline levels, and \(W\) contains the spatial weights. In a simultaneous specification the shocks and scales must be solved jointly. Existence and positivity depend on the weights and the innovation specification.

Interpreting local scale and diagnosing clustering

For simultaneous spatial feedback, \(h_i\) is a local scale parameter. Its equality to a conditional variance depends on the ordering and conditioning set. Moran’s \(I\) on squared residuals can reveal remaining clustering. Compare plausible mean specifications and weight matrices before attributing that pattern to spatial volatility.

Application to US county lung-cancer mortality

The authors fitted spatial mean models to county-level lung-cancer mortality and compared specifications with and without spatially dependent heteroscedastic errors. The example illustrates how a separate scale model can describe geographical variation left by the mean model.
